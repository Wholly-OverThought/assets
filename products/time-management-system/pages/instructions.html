How This System Works

Read this once. Then keep it nearby for the first week.

Before Anything Else Everything here — the Blocks, the check-ins, all of it — is optional. Use what helps. Leave what doesn't. This is a toolset, not an obligation.

The main purpose of this system is to find and create a routine. If you are the kind of person whose only consistent routine is routinely changing it, this would be more of a planner for you.

I always found it hard to craft a functional routine. As soon as I'd find something that would work, something would change, or I would.

As you may or may not know, I have been creating neurodivergent content for a while, and I create learning & productivity aids for myself to make life easier — possible, really — as I am neurodivergent myself.

I have lived with Asperger's and ADD, as well as C-PTSD, all of my adult life. Anyone living with any of these knows the most destructive product is the infusing of the identity with shame — so insidiously that we don't often feel shame, but instead have a deeply held, subconscious core belief: that I am very bad.

As such, healing from trauma and toxic shame is a matter of survival for us. I have always had a tremendous amount of shame, for various things, and didn't understand until recently that anyone who experiences trauma in childhood — many people do, really, to some degree — will have a shame problem. That is C-PTSD.

One shame area for me is from my inability to keep a normal schedule. First of all, just sleeping at night was an absolutely unscalable mountain. With mercy from God, I was able to start being able to be awake each and every morning. I am still working on the variance.

I am a scientist, but I am a follower of Christ also, along with being a mental health professional (certified Peer Support Specialist), so a system seemed like a good starting point. Then I knew I needed flexibility. But I knew I needed accountability.

The goal of the flexibility is to not hit the bad-button, or shame-spot, of any facet of my shame diamond in my abdomen. Each facet is from a different source or type.

This system is built out of Blocks — small groups of similar or related tasks (Hygiene, Body, Power Hour (a focused hour for completing 3 important tasks off your running to-do list), Home, Finance, Relationships, and whatever else you add). You arrange Blocks into your day in the order that makes sense. When the day goes sideways — and it will — you move Blocks around instead of abandoning them. A moved Block is not a missed Block.

Neuroscience, psychology, and older sources of wisdom — the Bible among them — agree on more than you'd expect about how the mind works, which is different than the brain. One point they converge on: grouping related things together makes each one easier to remember and easier to actually do. That's the whole mechanism behind a Block — similar tasks kept together so your mind isn't context-switching all day. But the point of this system isn't the Blocks themselves. It's using them, day after day, to find your optimal routine — and flexibility is what makes that routine something you can actually keep doing.

Finding that routine isn't a one-time setup, either. Any time your circumstances genuinely change — a new job with different hours, someone moving into your home, kids out of school and around all day — the steady routine you had stops working, not because you failed it, but because it was built for a life you don't have right now. Tweak it or overhaul it, depending on how big the change is. And if you're neurodivergent enough that no two days really look alike, there may be no “normal” to return to at all — if that's you, this was never meant to be a routine you set once. It's a routine-builder you use as often as you need it, however often that is.

Where This Came From I built the first version of this in prison, with eighteen to twenty-two hours a day and nothing in them. Writing down tiny lists — brush teeth, shave, shower — turned out to be the only thing that got me through. I have ADHD, I'm autistic, and I live with complex PTSD — though none of it had a name yet back in prison. Grouping small, concrete tasks together is still how I keep my own days from falling apart. ADHD, for me, isn't a willpower problem — it's closer to a brain that treats anything boring as a threat and won't be reasoned with about it. Small Blocks lower the cost of just starting. The flexibility exists because a schedule that can't survive one bad hour was never going to survive me. This was built to cut the shame of not finishing everything, not add to it.
The Core Idea It's not specificity that makes this work — “brush teeth,” not “hygiene,” was never really the point. The actual mechanism has three parts, all built around how an ADHD or autistic brain tends to work. First: if planning is hard — you forget, or avoid it — grouping similar tasks means you're only ever scheduling a handful of Blocks, not untangling your whole day, and those same Blocks are easy to move when plans change. Second: if a task feels too big to start, so you avoid it and then feel guilty, breaking it into small, concrete pieces removes the dread — there's no large thing left to avoid. Third: transitions are hard. Grouping similar tasks together means finishing one carries momentum into the next — it's the jump to something unrelated that creates the kind of transition most people's schedules don't account for, and that's exactly where avoidance and shame creep in. Put together: Blocks make the plan easier to build, easier to execute, and flexible enough that when something changes, the change is already part of the system — not proof you failed it.

1Set Your Blocks

Open the Blocks page. The tasks already filled in (Hygiene, Body, and the rest) are examples — they show you the shape of a Block, not a prescription for your life. Keep what fits, change what doesn't, delete the rest. If you'd rather start with nothing in your way, the Blank Blocks pages are built for exactly that.

2Lay Out Your Day

Each morning, use the Daily Organizer page to write your Blocks into the order you intend to run them. This is a plan, not a contract — it's written in pencil for a reason.

3Work the Blocks, Check In

Hourly — Prayer / Meditation

A 1–5 minute pause, roughly once an hour. Check or color in the next circle in the row on the Daily Organizer — one per hour, in order, left to right.

Every 15–30 Min — Mindfulness Alarm

A short reset: where am I, what am I doing, is it what I planned? Check or color in the next box in the grid — in sequence, not whichever one you like.

4When Something Interrupts You

Don't quit the day — reorganize it. Cross the Block out of its old spot on the Daily Organizer and write it into a new one. The goal is to finish your Blocks, not to finish them on schedule.

Most Important If you miss something, write it on the Skip Block so it doesn't just vanish — then let it go. This is a structured guide, not a rulebook. You will miss things. That's expected, not a failure.

5Morning & Evening

Short check-ins bookend the day on the Check-In page. (A full guided journal is a separate companion product, in development — this is just the light version that keeps the day anchored.)

6Going Portable

Two pages in this pack — Daily Organizer — Foldable and Blocks — Blank (Foldable) — are built to print on the front and back of one sheet. Print them double-sided, fold once down the center, and you've got the barebones system (block order, check-ins, blank blocks to define on the go) small enough to carry without the rest of the packet.
